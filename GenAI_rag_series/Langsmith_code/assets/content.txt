The Solar System is the gravitationally bound system made up of the Sun and everything that orbits it.
It formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the collapse of a giant cloud of gas and dust called a molecular cloud.
Most of the collapsing material gathered at the centre and became the Sun, while the rest flattened into a spinning disk.
Within this disk, small particles stuck together to form planetesimals, which slowly grew into planets.
The Sun contains about 99.86 percent of the total mass of the Solar System.
It is a G-type main-sequence star, often described as a yellow dwarf.
The Sun's core reaches temperatures of around 15 million degrees Celsius.
In the core, nuclear fusion converts hydrogen into helium and releases enormous amounts of energy.
Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.
The Sun's visible surface, called the photosphere, has a temperature of about 5,500 degrees Celsius.
Its outer atmosphere, the corona, is surprisingly much hotter, reaching more than a million degrees.
The Sun constantly releases a stream of charged particles known as the solar wind.
The solar wind carves out a vast bubble in space called the heliosphere.
There are eight officially recognised planets in the Solar System.
In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
The four inner planets are called terrestrial or rocky planets because they have solid surfaces.
The four outer planets are giant planets made mostly of gases and ices.
Jupiter and Saturn are known as gas giants, while Uranus and Neptune are called ice giants.
All eight planets orbit the Sun in the same direction and roughly in the same flat plane.
Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun.
A year on Mercury lasts only 88 Earth days.
Mercury has almost no atmosphere, so its temperature swings from about 430 degrees Celsius in the day to minus 180 at night.
Its surface is heavily cratered and looks similar to Earth's Moon.
Mercury has no moons.
Venus is the second planet from the Sun and is similar in size to Earth.
Venus is the hottest planet, with surface temperatures of around 465 degrees Celsius.
Its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat through a runaway greenhouse effect.
The atmospheric pressure on Venus is about 92 times that of Earth at sea level.
Venus spins very slowly and in the opposite direction to most planets.
A single day on Venus is longer than its year.
Venus is often the brightest object in the night sky after the Moon.
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only known world to support life.
About 71 percent of Earth's surface is covered by water.
Earth's atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, which helps protect life and regulate temperature.
Earth's magnetic field shields the planet from much of the harmful solar wind.
Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon.
The Moon is thought to have formed after a Mars-sized body collided with the early Earth.
The Moon's gravity is the main cause of the ocean tides on Earth.
Mars is the fourth planet and is known as the Red Planet.
Its red colour comes from iron oxide, or rust, in its soil and rocks.
Mars has a thin atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide.
Mars is home to Olympus Mons, the tallest volcano in the Solar System, about 22 kilometres high.
It also has Valles Marineris, a canyon system more than 4,000 kilometres long.
Evidence suggests that liquid water once flowed on the surface of Mars.
Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos.
Robotic rovers such as Curiosity and Perseverance have explored the Martian surface.
Between Mars and Jupiter lies the asteroid belt.
The asteroid belt contains millions of rocky objects left over from the formation of the Solar System.
The largest object in the asteroid belt is Ceres, which is classified as a dwarf planet.
Despite how they appear in films, the asteroids in the belt are very far apart from one another.
Jupiter is the fifth planet and the largest in the Solar System.
Jupiter is so big that more than 1,300 Earths could fit inside it.
It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, similar to the Sun.
Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a giant storm that has lasted for hundreds of years.
Jupiter has the shortest day of any planet, rotating once in about 10 hours.
Jupiter has a powerful magnetic field and dozens of moons, with new ones still being discovered.
Its four largest moons, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, are called the Galilean moons.
Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System and is even bigger than Mercury.
Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System.
Europa has an ocean beneath its icy crust that scientists think could potentially support life.
Saturn is the sixth planet and is famous for its spectacular ring system.
Saturn's rings are made of countless pieces of ice and rock, ranging from tiny grains to large boulders.
Saturn is the least dense planet and would float in water if there were a bathtub large enough.
Saturn has the most known moons of any planet in the Solar System.
Its largest moon, Titan, has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane and ethane.
Another moon, Enceladus, shoots jets of water vapour from an ocean hidden beneath its icy crust.
Uranus is the seventh planet and appears blue-green because of methane in its atmosphere.
Uranus is tilted on its side, with an axial tilt of about 98 degrees.
Because of this tilt, each pole gets about 42 years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 years of darkness.
Uranus was the first planet discovered with a telescope, found by William Herschel in 1781.
Neptune is the eighth and farthest known planet from the Sun.
Neptune was discovered in 1846 using mathematical predictions rather than chance observation.
Neptune has the strongest winds in the Solar System, reaching more than 2,000 kilometres per hour.
Its largest moon, Triton, orbits in the opposite direction to Neptune's rotation.
Triton is thought to be an object captured from the Kuiper Belt.
A year on Neptune lasts about 165 Earth years.
Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a wide region of icy bodies.
The Kuiper Belt is home to dwarf planets such as Pluto, Haumea and Makemake.
Pluto was considered the ninth planet until 2006, when it was reclassified as a dwarf planet.
The International Astronomical Union decided that a planet must clear its orbit of other objects, which Pluto does not do.
In 2015, NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto and revealed a heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice.
Eris, another dwarf planet, orbits far beyond Pluto and is nearly the same size.
Comets are icy bodies that develop glowing tails when they come close to the Sun.
Many short-period comets come from the Kuiper Belt.
Long-period comets are thought to come from the Oort Cloud.
The Oort Cloud is a distant, spherical shell of icy objects that may extend up to about 100,000 times the Earth-Sun distance.
Meteoroids are small rocky or metallic pieces that travel through space.
When a meteoroid burns up in Earth's atmosphere, it creates a streak of light called a meteor.
Pieces that survive the fall and land on the ground are called meteorites.
Distances in the Solar System are often measured in astronomical units, or AU.
One astronomical unit is the average distance between Earth and the Sun, about 150 million kilometres.
Neptune orbits at about 30 AU from the Sun.
The Voyager 1 spacecraft, launched in 1977, is the most distant human-made object.
Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in 2012.
The Solar System orbits the centre of the Milky Way galaxy once every 225 to 250 million years.
It is located in a minor spiral arm of the Milky Way called the Orion Arm.
In about 5 billion years, the Sun will run out of hydrogen in its core and swell into a red giant.
It may grow large enough to swallow Mercury, Venus and possibly Earth.
Eventually, the Sun will shed its outer layers and leave behind a small, dense white dwarf.
Studying the Solar System helps us understand how planets form and whether life might exist elsewhere in the universe.
